[ref Vijnana Bhairava Tantra: what the text is]
The Vijnana Bhairava Tantra is a short Sanskrit text of about 163 verses in the tradition of Kashmir Shaivism (it calls itself the essence of the Rudrayamala Tantra). It is a dialogue between the Goddess, Bhairavi (Devi), and Shiva as Bhairava. Its subject is how to recognise one's own true nature, the state called Bhairava: pure, open, aware being. Its special gift is practical: it gives 112 short "dharanas", ways of centering attention, many of them using ordinary moments of life (breathing, a sound, a joy, a sneeze, the sky) as doors to that awareness. Kashmir Shaiva teachers, notably Kshemaraja, quoted and commented on it. In the 20th century it became widely known through Swami Lakshman Joo's teaching and through modern books on meditation.

How the text is arranged:
- Verses 1-6: Devi's questions. She has heard the tantras, but her doubt remains: what is Bhairava's real nature? Is it the sound-energy of letters, the nine forms, the three powers, the moon-crescent and nada?
- Verses 7-23: Bhairava's answer. All those forms are steps for those not yet mature; the real Bhairava is beyond all divisions of place, time, form and thought, the state of fullness (bharita) that is one's own awareness. Shakti, his power, is not separate from him: she is the door to him.
- Verses 24-138: the 112 dharanas (ways of centering).
- Verses 139-163: the closing teaching: what true japa, worship, oblation and pilgrimage really are, and the end of the dialogue.

[ref Vijnana Bhairava Tantra: dharanas of the breath (from verse 24)]
The first dharanas use the breath. Verse 24: the out-breath rises (prana) and the in-breath descends (jiva/apana); by holding attention at the two places where each breath arises and turns, one reaches the state of fullness. Verses 25-26 continue: at the moment the breath turns, and in the stillness when breath neither goes out nor comes in, thought-free awareness (nirvikalpa) appears, and that is Bhairava's form. Verse 27 speaks of the peace that shines at the end of a retained breath. The common thread: the small gaps and turning points of breathing are not empty; they open onto awareness itself.

[ref Vijnana Bhairava Tantra: dharanas of space and emptiness (e.g. verses 32, 39, 42)]
Many dharanas use emptiness (shunya) and space. Verse 32: contemplating the five voids, like the five-coloured circles of a peacock's feather, one enters the supreme emptiness in the heart. Verses 39 and 42 use sound fading into emptiness: chanting a sound (such as Om or a seed syllable) and resting in the silence where it dissolves, one "becomes Shiva" through that empty sounding. Other verses ask one to feel the body, or the whole universe, as empty space. The point is not to think about emptiness but to rest in the open awareness that remains.

[ref Vijnana Bhairava Tantra: dharanas of the senses and everyday experience (e.g. verses 67, 71-73, 75, 84, 87, 118)]
Some of the best-known dharanas use ordinary experience:
- Verse 67: even the sensation of an ant crawling on the skin, attended to fully, opens supreme joy.
- Verse 71: when great joy arises, as on seeing a dear relative after a long time, meditate on that joy itself and become one with it.
- Verse 72: the delight of eating and drinking, attended to, becomes the state of fullness and great bliss.
- Verse 73: the joy of music and other beautiful things, when one becomes one with it, becomes the yogi's absorption.
- Verse 75: the moment between waking and sleep, when sleep has not yet come and outer awareness has faded, is where the supreme Devi shines.
- Verse 84: gazing steadily at the clear open sky, without moving the eyes, one attains Bhairava's form at once.
- Verse 87: dissolving into the deep darkness of a dark night, one attains Bhairava's form.
- Verse 118: at the beginning and end of a sneeze, in fear, in grief, at the edge of a deep pit, when fleeing a battle, in sudden curiosity, at the start and end of hunger: in such moments of shock, thought stops and the state of Brahman appears.
The teaching is that awareness is always present; strong or simple moments only reveal what is already there.

[ref Vijnana Bhairava Tantra: dharanas of the mind (e.g. verses 61, 101, 136, 138)]
Other dharanas work directly with the mind. Verse 61: when two thoughts or two perceptions arise, let go of both together and the reality between them shines. Verse 101: when desire, anger, greed, delusion, pride or envy arise, make the mind still and the underlying reality alone remains. Verse 136: withdrawing the senses, one rests in one's own Self. Verse 138: when the mind, the intellect, the life-force and the limited self dissolve, that is Bhairava's form.

[ref Vijnana Bhairava Tantra: true japa, worship and sacrifice (verses 139-163)]
In the closing verses Bhairava gives the inner meaning of religious practice:
- Verse 145: the real japa (repetition) is the self-arising inner sound itself; one does not have to manufacture it.
- Verse 147: real worship (puja) is not offering flowers but loving absorption of the mind in the thought-free vast space of awareness.
- Verse 148: real satisfaction (the result of offerings) is that state of utter fullness.
- Verse 149: the real fire-offering (homa) is when the elements, the senses and the mind are offered into the fire of the supreme Self, with awareness as the ladle.
- Verse 150 explains words of practice by their inner sense (what destroys all sins and protects all).
- Verse 153: the worshipper, the worshipped and worship are one; where then is a separate worship?
- Verse 154: the true holy place (kshetra) is the great Goddess within, the breath and life itself.
Devi is satisfied (verses 161-162: she says she has now grasped the essence of the Rudrayamala and the heart of all the powers), and the dialogue ends with Bhairava and Bhairavi in union.

[ref Vijnana Bhairava Tantra: how to explain it to a seeker]
When someone asks about the Vijnana Bhairava: explain that it offers many doors to the same room, and one need not practise all 112; one that suits the person, practised gently and regularly, is enough. Quote the Sanskrit verse when helpful, explain it simply, and do not invent verse numbers. Some dharanas (breath retention, intense states) should be practised gently and, for serious practice, with an experienced teacher. Verse 160 itself says this teaching is to be given only to those who are ready, and treated as precious.
