[ref Satyanarayan Katha: what it is]
The Satyanarayan Vrat Katha is the story recited at the Satyanarayan Puja, one of the most loved household worships in India. Satyanarayan is Lord Vishnu (Narayana) as Truth (Satya). The best-known version, recited in most homes, is said to come from the Skanda Purana, Reva Khanda (in the library: chapters 233-237, five chapters). A related version appears in the Bhavishya Purana (Pratisarga Parva 3.2.24-29). The message is simple: keep your word, do not forget a promise made to God, honour the prasad, and live truthfully; devotion is open to everyone, rich or poor, king or woodcutter.

[ref Satyanarayan Katha, Chapter 1: Narada's question and the vrat]
The sages in the Naimisha forest, led by Shaunaka, ask Suta how people can get what they wish for through a vow or austerity. Suta tells what Lord Vishnu himself told Narada. Narada, travelling the worlds out of compassion, saw people on earth suffering in many births because of their deeds, and went to Vishnu (shining, four-armed, holding conch, discus, mace and lotus) and praised him. Vishnu told him of an easy remedy for the Kali age: the Satyanarayan vrat.
How it is done (as the text and its Hindi explanation describe): on any day, especially on a full-moon day (Purnima) or Sankranti, in the evening, with devotion and with family and friends; worship Lord Satyanarayan; offer a prasad made in "sava" (one and a quarter) measure, of banana, ghee, milk and wheat flour with sugar (rice flour and jaggery may be used instead); listen to the katha; give dakshina to the brahmins and feed them and the relatives; then sing kirtan, remember Satyanarayan and go home. Whoever does this vrat with faith has their wishes fulfilled.

[ref Satyanarayan Katha, Chapter 2: the poor brahmin and the woodcutter]
In Kashi lived a very poor brahmin (named Shatananda, as chapter 5 recalls) who wandered begging. Out of kindness the Lord came to him in the form of an old brahmin and taught him the Satyanarayan vrat. He performed it the very next day, his poverty ended, and he did it every month. A woodcutter, seeing the brahmin's worship and hearing of its fruit, did the vrat too with what he earned that day from selling wood; he too became prosperous and happy. Lesson: the worship needs faith, not wealth.

[ref Satyanarayan Katha, Chapter 3: King Ulkamukha, the merchant Sadhu, his broken vow and the prison]
King Ulkamukha and his queen were performing the vrat (by a river, in the usual telling) when a merchant named Sadhu came by boat and asked about it. He vowed to do the vrat if he were blessed with a child. A daughter, Kalavati, was born to his wife Lilavati, but Sadhu kept postponing his vow: first "at her marriage", and when she married, he forgot again. He went trading with his son-in-law to the city of Ratnasarapura (King Chandraketu's city); there they were falsely accused of stealing the king's wealth and thrown into prison. At home, Lilavati and Kalavati fell into poverty; Kalavati saw the Satyanarayan vrat being done in another house, and mother and daughter performed it. The Lord then appeared to King Chandraketu in a dream, and the merchant and his son-in-law were set free with honour. Lesson: a promise made to God must be kept.

[ref Satyanarayan Katha, Chapter 4: the boat of "creepers and leaves" and Kalavati's prasad]
On the way home the Lord came as an ascetic (dandi) and asked what was in the boat; Sadhu lied that it carried only creepers and leaves (latapatra), and so it became. When he repented and prayed, the wealth returned. When the boat reached home, Kalavati, in her joy to see her husband, ran to the shore without taking the prasad, and the boat with her husband sank from sight; when she went back and took the prasad with reverence, he was seen again. Lesson: speak the truth, and never disrespect prasad.

[ref Satyanarayan Katha, Chapter 5: King Tungadhvaja and the cowherds, and later births]
King Tungadhvaja came upon cowherds doing the Satyanarayan puja; out of pride he neither bowed nor took the prasad they offered, and he lost his sons and wealth. Realising his mistake, he went back, worshipped with the cowherds and took the prasad, and everything was restored; he ruled happily and attained the Lord's abode. The chapter ends by telling the later births of those who did this vrat: the poor brahmin Shatananda was born as Sudama (Krishna's friend); King Ulkamukha as King Dasharatha; the merchant Sadhu as King Moradhvaja; King Tungadhvaja as Svayambhuva Manu; and the woodcutter as Guharaj (Guha, the Nishada king who served Shri Rama). Lesson: God's grace does not look at caste, wealth or rank; it looks at humility and truth.

[ref Satyanarayan Katha: how to explain it]
When someone asks about the Satyanarayan katha or puja: tell the story simply, chapter by chapter, with its lessons (truthfulness, keeping one's word, respect for prasad, humility); explain the puja steps in general terms; and say that family customs differ (the day, the prasad, the order of worship), so follow the family's or the priest's tradition where it exists. Quote the Sanskrit verses from the library when helpful, with their chapter and verse number.
